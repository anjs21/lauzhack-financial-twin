MDP Action Shortlist

We simulated 29 decisions and shocks on all 1,200 synthetic profiles over ten years and counted whose outcome each one changes. Four levers carry nearly all of the controllable effect: work, housing cost, the cash buffer and the spending level. Debt actions barely matter in this data. The largest swings come from life choices (starting a business, cutting to 80% hours, buying a car, a master's), and those only belong in the policy's choice set if the reward has an explicit preference term.

The answer

What goes into the MDP

The policy chooses among four levers. The user fixes a life-choice branch, the environment draws shocks, and a few catalogue items can be dropped or folded into a lever. Each card gives the strongest piece of evidence for including it.

Work

Agent lever
  • Take a full-time job (student, unemployed)
  • Part-time → full-time
  • Search takes months; success is a chance event

Income accounts for 77–94% of how outcomes differ between profiles. A job fixes 72 of 76 student and unemployed shortfalls; full-time work helps all 120 part-timers.

Housing cost

Agent lever
  • Flat 20% cheaper
  • Shared flat (single households)
  • Cheapest-rent city (St Gallen here)

The best financial lever for 671 of 1,200 profiles, and it fixes 38 shortfalls. Rent is the only large cost that varies between profiles.

Cash buffer

Agent lever
  • Target buffer: 1, 3 or 6 months of outgoings
  • Invest above it; sell investments below 1 month
  • Applied every month, so it acts as a rule

Investing excess cash once leaves 150 better off and 149 worse off. With the sell-when-low rule: 233 better, 28 worse, and 33 shortfalls fixed.

Spending level

Agent lever
  • Non-rent costs −10%, unchanged or +10%
  • About CHF 150 a month either way
  • Needs a preference cost, or −10% always wins

Small but universal: it materially changes 21% of profiles and never hurts, so on cash alone the policy would always pick −10%.

How the tiers were set: an intervention is Tier 1 if it changes the outcome for at least 20% of all profiles, or fixes at least a quarter of the 150 baseline shortfalls; Tier 2 if it changes at least 5% of all profiles or 40% of its eligible group. "Changes the outcome" means a shift of 10 points or more in shortfall risk or goal odds, or of 10% in 2034 net worth.

Evidence · the data

What drives cash flow in the data

The synthetic ledger was generated by a simple rule, and it can be decoded. Once the rule is known, the transition model calibrates itself. Income dominates. Living costs are almost the same for everyone (about CHF 1,480 a month, whatever the household size), so rent is the only cost lever with real spread.

Evidence · simulation

Which interventions change outcomes

Each intervention is applied in January 2025 to every eligible profile, and compared with continuing as-is under the same random shocks. Bars show the share of all 1,200 profiles that end up materially better or worse off, so levers that apply to small groups (a job for the 76 students and unemployed) look short here; the next section splits them by group. Evidence · who it helps

Each lever works for a different group

Employed people almost never run short, so their decisions are about wealth. Part-timers and retirees carry real risk that housing and liquidity moves reduce. Students and unemployed people run short whatever else they do, unless they start working.

Best financial lever per profile

For each profile, the lever that most lowers shortfall risk, then raises goal odds, then raises 2034 net worth. A certain pay rise is excluded because its outcome is not the person's to choose.

Evidence · why an MDP

The liquidity action has to be a rule

Moving spare cash into investments once looks attractive on net worth, but it raises shortfall risk for anyone whose cash later runs down, retirees most of all (+16 points). Add one state-dependent rule, selling investments when cash drops below a month of outgoings, and the same move becomes safe. This is the clearest case in the data where a closed-loop policy beats any one-shot scenario.

Evidence · state

Which state variables matter

A random forest predicted each profile's baseline outcome from its starting state, and each variable was then shuffled to see how much accuracy it carries. Income dominates every outcome. Rent comes next; investments matter for goals, and cash matters for timing. Age, household, city and debt add almost nothing beyond eligibility, so they can stay out of the transition state.

Evidence · validation

Can the simulator be trusted?

The transition model was calibrated on 2025 only and asked to predict 2026, starting from each profile's recorded December 2025 cash. It reproduces which profiles run short, and its uncertainty bands have the right width.

Transitions

Fitted from the ledger: pay drift +1.42% a year, cost inflation +1.01%, noise, one-off bills, December bonus and student fees (all in the card above). Assumed and labelled: investment returns of 4% a year with 10% volatility, 60-month loan annuities, 8% a year on negative cash, and job-search delays. The shocks need assumed rates: costs outpacing pay, rent rises, spells out of work, market drawdowns and emergency bills.

Reward

r_t = − λ_short · 1[cash_t < 0]  − λ_gap · max(0, −cash_t) / 10k
      + B_high · 1[high goal met, Dec 2029, paid once]  + B_med · 1[medium goal met, Dec 2032, paid once]
      + λ_wealth · Δnet_worth_t / 100k  − κ · 1[a_t ≠ continue]  + u_branch

starting values to tune and show the user:
  λ_short = 1   λ_gap = 0.5   B_high = 10   B_med = 5   λ_wealth = 1   κ = 0.05   u_branch = user-set

Loopholes this run found

  1. Free overdraft. The ledger lets cash go negative at no cost, so holding investments while overdrawn beats selling. Charge interest (8% a year here) or forbid it.
  2. Free improvements. A certain 10% pay rise and a costless spending cut help every profile, so an agent always takes them. Make raises a chance event and give spending cuts a preference cost.
  3. Life choices never pay in cash. Without a preference term the agent never studies, starts a business, retires early or has a child. Fix the branch first, then optimise the levers inside it.
  4. Students study forever. Their do-nothing path is ten more years on a student budget. Add a graduation event or force a work decision.
  5. Structure the data lacks. There is no household-size effect on spending, no pay rise with age and no city effect on pay. Child costs, career progression and relocation pay are assumptions and should be labelled as such.
